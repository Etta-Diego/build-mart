# Redis Cache Effectiveness Experiment — Chapter 4 Write-Up

## Methodology

To determine whether Redis caching reduces end-to-end client-perceived response latency for the Enhanced Microservices architecture, an interleaved repeated-measures experiment was conducted against the `product-service` `GET /api/products/featured` endpoint (Amazon EKS, cache-aside pattern, Amazon ElastiCache for Redis, TTL 300 seconds). An earlier, single-block pilot (sequential MISS-then-HIT phases, N=12/N=100) had identified a time-order confound — condition was perfectly correlated with elapsed test time, leaving process and connection warm-up as a plausible alternative explanation for the observed effect. This experiment supersedes that pilot with a design that eliminates the confound directly.

Thirty cycles were executed, each consisting of: (1) deletion of the `featured_products` Redis key with confirmed absence (`EXISTS=0`); (2) a single MISS request via k6; (3) confirmation of cache population (`EXISTS`, `TYPE`, `TTL`, `MEMORY USAGE`); (4) a randomized batch of 8–12 HIT requests (uniform, chosen per cycle to avoid a fixed-period aliasing risk); and (5) a final cache-state check. All HTTP requests used `Connection: close`, forcing a fresh TCP and TLS handshake on every request in both conditions, symmetrically removing connection-reuse and keep-alive advantages as a confound; this was verified directly from per-request transport metrics (non-zero `http_req_connecting` and `http_req_tls_handshaking` on every request) rather than assumed. Traffic originated from an EC2 k6 runner inside `eu-west-3`; Redis state was verified independently via `kubectl exec` into the product-service pod, using the pod's own `ioredis` client and `REDIS_URL` (never read directly). Redis's `INFO stats` counters (`keyspace_hits`/`keyspace_misses`) were also recorded at each checkpoint as supporting evidence only: the ElastiCache instance was confirmed (via SHA-256 comparison of `REDIS_URL`) to be shared with `cart-service`, so these counters are instance-wide and cannot establish per-request causality — that link is established through the key-specific `EXISTS`/`TTL` state transitions instead. HPA replica count, pod CPU/memory, and product-service logs were captured before and after the experiment to confirm autoscaling was not a confound.

Statistical analysis used Python (pandas 3.0.5, numpy 2.5.1, scipy 1.18.0). Because the design produces paired observations (one MISS latency and one mean-of-batch HIT latency per cycle), the primary analysis is a Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the 30 paired cycle-level observations, with a paired bootstrap 95% confidence interval (10,000 resamples) on the mean difference and Cohen's dz as the effect size. A secondary, pooled request-level analysis (Mann-Whitney U, Welch's t-test) is reported for comparability with the earlier pilot's framing, explicitly labelled as treating clustered, non-independent observations as independent, and is not the primary inferential result. A Spearman correlation between cycle index and per-cycle mean latency was computed for both conditions as a direct test for residual time-order drift.

## Results

The corrected dataset comprises N=30 MISS observations and N=297 HIT observations (one dry-run cycle used for pipeline validation was excluded; two cycles experienced transient SSH/`kubectl` connectivity incidents that compromised only a corroborating verification step in each case, not the underlying latency measurements, and are documented in full in the accompanying experiment metadata).

**Descriptive statistics.** MISS requests had a mean latency of 21.152ms (SD=7.557, median=19.197, p90=31.309, p95=37.679, p99=42.592, 95% CI [18.330, 23.974]). HIT requests had a mean latency of 14.782ms (SD=6.307, median=13.592, p90=21.935, p95=26.174, p99=33.469, 95% CI [14.062, 15.502]).

**Primary analysis.** The paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the 30 cycle-level pairs was statistically significant (W=55.0, p=0.0001). The mean paired difference (MISS − mean HIT per cycle) was 6.164ms (SD=8.198), corresponding to an effect size of dz=0.752 (a medium-to-large effect by conventional benchmarks). A paired bootstrap 95% confidence interval on the mean difference was [3.320, 9.119]ms, excluding zero.

**Secondary analysis.** The pooled request-level comparison (Mann-Whitney U=7228.0, p<0.000001; Welch's t=4.462, df=33.208, p=0.0001; pooled bootstrap 95% CI [3.831, 9.301]ms) corroborates the primary result, though this analysis treats within-cycle-clustered HIT observations as independent and is reported only as a robustness check.

**Drift check.** Spearman correlation between cycle index and per-cycle mean latency was not significant for either condition (MISS: ρ=-0.116, p=0.540; HIT: ρ=-0.290, p=0.120), providing no evidence of residual time-order drift and supporting the internal validity of the interleaved design.

**Overall effect.** Mean latency reduction: **30.12%**. Median latency reduction: **29.20%**.

HPA replica count remained at 2/2 throughout (CPU utilisation 0–1%), confirming autoscaling did not confound the measurement.

## Discussion

The result confirms, with a statistically defensible and internally valid design, that the cache-aside pattern produces a real, substantial reduction in client-perceived latency for this endpoint. The effect size (dz=0.752) and the consistency between the primary paired test and the secondary pooled robustness check both support this conclusion.

The magnitude (≈30% mean reduction) is notably smaller than the ≈59% reported by the earlier single-block pilot, and the shift moved in both directions: MISS latency was lower here (21.2ms vs. 28.4ms) and HIT latency was higher (14.8ms vs. 11.6ms) than in the pilot. This experiment's own evidence offers a plausible, though not conclusively proven, account: product-service pods had accumulated 7 days 22 hours of uptime at validation time, which would tend to lower MISS cost further through warmed connection pools and JIT-optimised request handling; and the shared ElastiCache instance's `keyspace_hits` counter already exceeded 663,000 before this experiment began, confirming substantially more background load (from `cart-service` and/or other traffic sharing the instance) than whatever state prevailed during the pilot, which would tend to raise HIT latency under contention. Both directions of the observed shift are consistent with this account, though it should be read as the most evidence-consistent explanation available, not a proven causal mechanism — no direct, isolated measurement of "Redis instance load at pilot time" exists for comparison. This discrepancy is itself informative: it illustrates that a cache-effectiveness measurement taken at one point on a shared, live, multi-tenant cache instance is not necessarily stable over time, and that fixing the order confound also, incidentally, moved the measurement to a different point in the system's operational history.

## Limitations

- Results are scoped to a single endpoint (`/api/products/featured`) and a single connection-per-request HTTP pattern (`Connection: close`); they should not be generalised to concurrent, keep-alive production traffic or to other cached endpoints without a dedicated follow-up.
- Requests were load-balanced across two running replica pods without per-request pod attribution; residual cross-pod variability cannot be fully excluded, though the interleaved design distributes any such variability across both conditions rather than concentrating it in one.
- The ElastiCache instance is confirmed shared with `cart-service`; `keyspace_hits`/`keyspace_misses` are therefore coarse, instance-wide supporting evidence only, not per-request proof — the primary evidence for individual miss/hit events remains the key-specific `EXISTS`/`TYPE`/`TTL` state transitions.
- Two cycles (05, 21) experienced transient SSH/`kubectl` connectivity incidents. In both cases the underlying latency measurements were recovered intact and verified (12/12 checks passed in each affected HIT batch), but the corroborating post-batch Redis-state confirmation is missing (Cycle 05) or was rendered uninformative by a 12-minute delay that allowed natural TTL expiry (Cycle 21) rather than reflecting anything about the HIT requests themselves. These are documented explicitly rather than silently excluded or smoothed over.
- Total wall-clock runtime (44m36s) exceeded the ~15–20 minute design target, driven by the same two connectivity incidents; this did not affect the experiment's internal validity (the drift check found no significant time-order effect) but is noted as a practical deviation from the planned protocol.

## Threats to Validity

**Internal validity.** The interleaved, paired design and the non-significant Spearman drift test directly address the time-order confound identified in the earlier pilot. A discarded warm-up request preceded Cycle 1 to prevent a one-time infrastructure cold-path delay (observed at ~22 seconds during pre-flight checks) from contaminating the first measurement.

**Construct validity.** The measured `http_req_duration` represents full client-perceived latency (TCP, TLS, API Gateway, NLB, Kubernetes routing, application processing, and — on MISS — MongoDB and Redis operations), not an isolated Redis-versus-MongoDB server-side delta. This is the intended measurement (what a real client experiences) but should not be mistaken for a component-level benchmark.

**External validity.** Findings are scoped to this single endpoint, this connection pattern, and the operational conditions (pod uptime, shared-instance background load) present at the time of measurement, which this experiment has shown are not necessarily stable over time.
